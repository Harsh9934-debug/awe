6. Our Approach
Eyebrow: Our Approach
Human First by Design
The tools will keep changing. Our approach gives people a way to keep learning: practise on real tasks, question the output and take responsibility for the decision.
6.1 Practical AI Skills
Build confidence through relevant examples and hands-on practice. Learn how to frame a task, write useful prompts, evaluate responses and apply AI where it adds value.
6.2 Conscious Leadership
Connect AI adoption to purpose, responsibility and the people affected by it. Explore how to lead change, communicate clearly and make considered choices about what to automate, where to experiment and when to involve others.
6.3 Awareness and Human Wisdom
Our approach draws on neuroscience and behavioural research, alongside ancient wisdom traditions. We bring practical AI learning together with reflection, meditation and optional breathwork practices, with the aim of cultivating attention, emotional awareness and more considered decisions. These practices create opportunities to pause, notice assumptions and consider another perspective before acting. They complement practical skills, evidence and professional judgement.
Learn Through Deliberate Practice
01 — Clarify the challenge

Define the decision, context and what a useful result would look like.

02 — Explore with AI

Ask questions, develop options and test alternative perspectives.

03 — Question the answer

Check evidence, assumptions, omissions and consequences.

04 — Decide and reflect

Make your own judgement, act responsibly and learn from the result.
What Informs the Approach
Research has explored how meditation and structured breathing affect attention, stress, mood and aspects of decision-making. Findings vary across practices, settings and participants. We use this research to inform how we teach, while recognising that a change in mood or brain activity does not by itself demonstrate better business decisions.

AWE Intelligence’s combined approach has not been independently validated as a programme. Our aim is to help people develop useful habits of attention and reflection alongside their AI skills.

9. Research Behind Our Approach

These studies inform the rationale for attention and reflection practices. They do not validate AWE Intelligence’s programme or establish that breathwork and meditation reliably improve workplace decisions.
9.1 Attention and Self-Regulation
Tang and colleagues (2007) randomly assigned 80 Chinese undergraduates to five days of integrative body–mind training or relaxation training. The meditation group improved on a measure of executive attention and showed differences in mood and stress responses. The study tested a specific, supported training protocol, not a brief workplace exercise or business decision performance.

Source: Tang et al. 2007 — Short-term meditation training improves attention and self-regulation
9.2 Brain Networks Involved in Executive Control
Taren and colleagues (2017) studied 35 stressed unemployed adults assigned to a three-day mindfulness or relaxation programme. They reported differences in resting-state functional connectivity involving executive-control networks. This small neuroimaging study offers a possible mechanism to investigate; it did not establish improved executive performance or better real-world decisions.

Source: Taren et al. 2017 — Mindfulness meditation training and executive control network connectivity
9.3 Structured Breathing and Emotional State
Balban and colleagues (2023) studied daily five-minute breathing exercises and mindfulness meditation over 28 days. The trial found improvements in mood and reductions in respiratory rate, with particularly promising findings for exhale-focused cyclic sighing. Decision quality was not measured. These results support exploring structured breathing for emotional state, not a claim that it produces better decisions.

Source: Balban et al. 2023 — Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal
9.4 Mindfulness and a Specific Decision Bias
Hafenbrack, Kinias and Barsade (2014) reported that a brief mindfulness exercise reduced susceptibility to sunk-cost bias in experimental decision scenarios. This concerns one kind of bias under study conditions, rather than general decision-making ability. Evidence is mixed. A 2022 study using a different intervention design did not find mindfulness superior to relaxation on sunk-cost bias. Duration, comparison condition and setting matter, so neither result should be presented as a universal effect.

Sources: Hafenbrack et al. 2014 — Debiasing the mind through meditation; 2022 counterevidence — Meditation in the workplace
